\begin{center}\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}\small
\textbf{Abstract.} We study a three-parameter family of purely fermionic gauge quantum mechanics: $p$ flavours of
adjoint fermions for a rank-$r$ gauge algebra, with a nilpotent supercharge of odd degree $q$. The BPS states are
the cohomology of $Q$, which acts on the Fock space as multiplication by a fixed $q$-form $\omega\in\Lambda^qV$.
We find that the BPS states occupy a window of consecutive R-charges whose width grows \emph{linearly in the
rank}, $W=r(w_s-1)+1$ at the maximal weight and $W\ge r(2\min(p,q-1)-p)+1$ at every weight for $u(N)$.
Since the irreducible complexes are graded by $N_\Psi$ mod $q$, a window wider than $q$ cannot place one degree
per class, and R-charge concentration fails for all $r$ beyond a fixed bound. The mechanism is identifiable:
gauge invariance forces every interaction term to carry zero weight, and the positive-root subspace --- half of
any reductive Lie algebra --- admits no such term. The resulting interaction hypergraph is sparse, with
transversal density $\tau/n\to\frac12$, whereas ungauged SYK has $\tau/n\to1$ and is unobstructed. The obstruction
is therefore attributable to gauge invariance itself, not to the flavour count, the choice of couplings, or the
loss of Casimir solvability. Fortuity, by contrast, responds to gauge invariance in the \emph{opposite} way: the
stabilisation map $H_N\to H_{N+1}$ vanishes identically, because embedding creates a new site whose self-loop is
necessarily unoccupied. All statements are established for $u(N)$ and verified against exact integer computations
for $N\le5$; the $\su(N)$ case is discussed and left open.
\end{minipage}\end{center}

\section{The models}\label{sec:model}

Let $\g$ be a reductive Lie algebra of rank $r$ with a fixed invariant form, and let $\Psi^a$, $a=1,\dots,p$, be
complex fermionic fields valued in $\g$. For $\g=\gl(N)$ we write $\Psi^a_{ij}$ with $i,j=1,\dots,N$ and
\begin{equation}
\{\Psi^a_{ij},\bar\Psi^b_{kl}\}=\delta^{ab}\delta_{il}\delta_{jk},
\end{equation}
the gauge group acting by conjugation. The Fock space is the exterior algebra
\begin{equation}
\mathcal H=\Lambda^\bullet V,\qquad V=\mathbb C^p\otimes\g,\qquad n\equiv\dim V=p\dim\g ,
\end{equation}
graded by the R-charge $k=N_\Psi=\Tr\Psi^a\bar\Psi^a$, so that $\Lambda^kV$ is the $k$-fermion sector, of
dimension $\binom nk$. We call a basis vector of $V$ a \emph{mode}; a basis vector of $\Lambda^kV$ is then a set
$S$ of $k$ modes, and we write $m_S=\prod_{v\in S}v$ for the corresponding state. The wedge product is the
anticommuting product, so that $v\wedge v=0$ is Pauli exclusion written algebraically. All dynamics comes from a
single supercharge,
\begin{equation}
Q=\sum_{a_1\dots a_q}C_{a_1\dots a_q}\,\Tr\!\big[\Psi^{a_1}\cdots\Psi^{a_q}\big],\qquad H=\{Q,\bar Q\}\ \ge0,
\qquad [N_\Psi,Q]=q\,Q .
\label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}

Only \emph{odd} $q$ is admissible, and for two independent reasons. A cyclic shift inside $\Tr[\Psi^q]$ costs
$(-1)^{q-1}$, so the trace vanishes identically for even $q$; and $Q^2=0$ requires that exchanging the two
$q$-tuples in $C\otimes C$ --- a permutation of $2q$ fermions built from $q^2$ transpositions, contracted against
a totally antisymmetric product --- produce a relative minus sign, which again happens precisely for odd $q$.
The case $(p,q)=(1,3)$ is Chen's exactly solvable single-matrix model, in which $H=3N(N^2-1)-9\hat C_2$ is a
function of the gauge Casimir alone; $(p,q)=(3,3)$ is his proposed ``matrix SYK'' model, for which that Casimir
structure is lost.

The structural fact that organises everything below is that $Q$ is multiplication by a form. Every $\Psi$ is a
creation operator, so $Q$ contains no annihilators whatsoever and acts on $\Lambda^\bullet V$ as nothing more
than left multiplication:
\begin{equation}
Q=\omega\wedge-\,,\qquad \omega=\sum C_{a_1\dots a_q}\Tr[\Psi^{a_1}\cdots\Psi^{a_q}]\in\Lambda^qV ,
\qquad H^\bullet=\operatorname{Ann}(\omega)/(\omega).
\label{eq:wedge}
\end{equation}
For $p=1$ the form $\Tr\Psi^3$ is proportional to the Cartan $3$-form $\Tr(x[y,z])$ of $\g$. The entire problem is
thus the cohomology of multiplication by one specific, highly non-generic element of $\Lambda^qV$, and the
question of R-charge concentration becomes a question about where in the exterior algebra that multiplication
fails to be injective or surjective.

The Cartan torus acts on $\Psi_{ij}$ by $u_iu_j^{-1}$, so that mode carries weight $e_i-e_j$ and a state's weight
is the sum over its modes, i.e.\ the row sums minus the column sums of the occupation matrix $n_{ij}$. Diagonal
modes carry $e_i-e_i=0$ and are invisible to the weight. Because $Q$ is a gauge singlet it preserves the weight,
and because $[N_\Psi,Q]=qQ$ it raises $k$ by $q$; the cohomology therefore splits into irreducible complexes
labelled by
\begin{equation}
(\lambda,\ \omega_{\rm fl},\ c),\qquad \lambda\in\widehat{U(N)},\quad \omega_{\rm fl}\in\mathbb Z_p,\quad
c=k \bmod q .
\end{equation}
R-charge concentration, in the sense of Chang, Chen, Sia and Yang, is the statement that in each complex of
macroscopic index all BPS states sit at a single degree. Writing $W$ for the number of consecutive R-charges that
carry BPS states, a necessary condition is therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\ W\le q\ }
\label{eq:concentration}
\end{equation}
since a window wider than the grading period must, by pigeonhole, place two occupied degrees in one residue class.
Everything that follows is an estimate of $W$.

\section{The maximal irrep: index loops and the slot factorisation}\label{sec:max}

\subsection{The maximal weight}

The highest weight appearing in the Fock space is found greedily: fill row $1$ and empty column $1$, then do the
same with row $2$, and so on. The result is the fully upper-triangular occupation, with
\begin{equation}
\lambda^*_i=p\,(N+1-2i),\qquad
C_2^{\max}=\tfrac12 p(p+1)\textstyle\sum_i(N+1-2i)^2=\frac{p(p+1)N(N^2-1)}{6},
\label{eq:lamstar}
\end{equation}
where we used $\lambda^*_i+N+1-2i=(p+1)(N+1-2i)$ together with $\sum_i(N+1-2i)^2=N(N^2-1)/3$. At $p=1$ this
reduces to $N(N^2-1)/3$, which is Chen's $C_2(r_*)$, so \eqref{eq:lamstar} is its multi-flavour generalisation; at
$p=3$ it gives $2N(N^2-1)=48,120,240$ for $N=3,4,5$. Shifting $\lambda^*$ to a partition gives row lengths
$p(2N-2i)$ --- exactly $p$ times Chen's staircase, with the same $N-1$ non-empty rows. Adding flavours stretches
the diagram sideways without adding a single row, a point we return to in \S\ref{sec:fortuity}.

\subsection{Index loops}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.93\textwidth]{report_figs/g_loops.pdf}
\caption{(a) At the maximal weight the occupation is forced: every mode with $i<j$ is full, every mode with $i>j$
is empty, and only the diagonal is free. (b) Reading modes as coloured directed edges, $Q$ creates a closed walk
$i\to j\to k\to i$ and needs all its edges empty. Uphill edges are occupied, so every creatable step is
non-increasing; a cycle of non-increasing steps must be constant, leaving only self-loops.}
\label{fig:loops}
\end{figure}

At $\lambda^*$ the off-diagonal sector is completely rigid --- every upper mode occupied, every lower mode empty
(Fig.~\ref{fig:loops}a) --- so the weight space is just an exterior algebra on the zero-weight modes,
\begin{equation}
(\Lambda V)_{\lambda^*}\cong\Lambda^\bullet(\mathcal Z),\qquad \mathcal Z=\text{Cartan}\otimes\mathbb C^p,
\qquad\dim\mathcal Z=p\,r .
\end{equation}
What survives of $Q$ on this space is severely restricted, and the restriction is best seen graphically. Think of
a mode $\Psi^a_{ij}$ as a directed edge $i\to j$ carrying colour $a$. A term of $Q$ is a trace, so the $q$ modes
it creates form a closed walk $i_1\to i_2\to\cdots\to i_1$, and by Pauli exclusion the term acts non-trivially
only if every edge of that walk is currently empty. But at the maximal weight the empty modes are exactly those
with $i>j$, together with whatever diagonal flavours happen to be free. Every step of a creatable walk therefore
satisfies $i_t\ge i_{t+1}$: the walk may descend or stay level, but it can never climb, because all the uphill
edges are already occupied. Closing the walk forces $i_1\ge i_2\ge\cdots\ge i_1$, hence $i_1=\cdots=i_q$, and the
$q$ modes must be distinct and so carry $q$ distinct flavours. \textbf{Only self-loops survive}: $q$ diagonal
modes of distinct flavours sitting at a single site. We have checked this exhaustively at $N=3$, $p=3$, where all
$192$ non-zero actions of $Q$ on the maximal-weight space are of exactly this form.

\subsection{Factorisation and the width formula}

Since every surviving term sits at one site, $Q$ splits as $Q=\sum_{i=1}^r Q_i$ with $Q_i$ acting only inside site
$i$'s flavour space. The $q$ creation operators of a self-loop all live at the same site and anticommute, so only
the totally antisymmetric part of the coupling tensor survives and
\begin{equation}
Q_i=\omega_{\rm sl}\wedge-\ \text{ on }\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^p,\qquad \omega_{\rm sl}=\Alt(C)\in\Lambda^q\mathbb C^p .
\end{equation}
The $Q_i$ act on disjoint tensor factors of $\Lambda(\mathcal Z)=\big(\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^p\big)^{\otimes r}$,
so K\"unneth applies and the Poincar\'e polynomials simply multiply:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\ Z_{\max}(t)=t^{k_0}\big(z_{\rm slot}(t)\big)^{r}\ },\qquad
z_{\rm slot}(t)=\sum_j\dim H^j\big(\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^p,\ \omega_{\rm sl}\wedge\big)t^j ,
\label{eq:kunneth}
\end{equation}
with $k_0=p|\Delta^+|$ counting the frozen off-diagonal modes. Multiplying $r$ copies of a polynomial adds their
degree-spans, so if $z_{\rm slot}$ is supported on $w_s$ degrees then
\begin{equation}
W=r\,(w_s-1)+1 .
\label{eq:Wformula}
\end{equation}
This is the width of the BPS window at the maximal weight, in closed form, for every $(p,q,N)$.

It is worth seeing the machinery run once. Take $p=q=3$ and $N=3$. The slot algebra $\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^3$
has dimensions $1,3,3,1$, and $\Alt(C)$ spans the one-dimensional $\Lambda^3\mathbb C^3$, so $\omega_{\rm
sl}\wedge$ is non-zero only as the map $\Lambda^0\to\Lambda^3$. Hence $H^0=0$ because that map is injective,
$H^1=H^2=3$ because the differential neither enters nor leaves those degrees, and $H^3=1-1=0$; that is,
$z_{\rm slot}=3t+3t^2=3t(1+t)$. Cubing and restoring the prefactor gives
$Z_{\max}=t^9\big(3t(1+t)\big)^3=27t^{12}+81t^{13}+81t^{14}+27t^{15}$, i.e.\ $h^{12..15}=27,81,81,27$, in exact
agreement with the computed spectrum in Table~\ref{tab:profiles}.

\subsection{The slot is never one degree wide}

Everything now hinges on $w_s$, and a short argument shows it can never be $1$. Grade $\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^p$
by $j$ mod $q$ and let $E_c=\sum_{j\equiv c}(-1)^j\binom pj$ be the Euler characteristic of class $c$; whenever
$E_c\ne0$ there must be cohomology somewhere in that class. Now $\sum_cE_c=(1-1)^p=0$, so it is impossible for
exactly one $E_c$ to be non-zero. On the other hand, writing $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/q}$ and resolving the constraint,
$E_c=\frac1q\sum_{m=1}^{q-1}\zeta^{-mc}(1-\zeta^m)^p$ with every $1-\zeta^m\ne0$, so they cannot all vanish
either. At least two classes must therefore carry cohomology, and
\begin{equation}
w_s\ge2\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad W\ge r+1 \quad\text{at the maximal weight.}
\label{eq:wsbound}
\end{equation}
Comparing with \eqref{eq:concentration}, concentration already requires $\rank G\le q-1$ --- a bound on the gauge
group that is independent of the flavour content entirely.

Computing $z_{\rm slot}$ for generic $\omega_{\rm sl}$ at $q=3$ gives $w_s=2,3,2,3,4,3,2,3$ for $p=1,\dots,8$,
periodic with period four, so that
\begin{equation}
W=N+1\ \ (p\equiv3\ \mathrm{mod}\ 4\text{, and }p=1),\qquad 2N+1\ \ (p\text{ even}),\qquad
3N+1\ \ (p\equiv1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 4,\ p>1).
\end{equation}
Two degenerations deserve comment. Whenever $q>p$ the space $\Lambda^q\mathbb C^p$ vanishes, the slot differential
dies, and $z_{\rm slot}=(1+t)^p$: pushing $q$ past the flavour count \emph{widens} the window to its maximum
rather than narrowing it. And $\dim\Lambda^3\mathbb C^3=1$, so at $p=q=3$ the coupling $\omega_{\rm sl}$ is unique
up to scale --- Chen's tensor is the only non-degenerate three-flavour cubic supercharge there is, and
randomising $C$ can only reproduce it or destroy it. Neither knob offers an escape.

\section{Triangles and critical sets}\label{sec:critical}

The width formula \eqref{eq:Wformula} is derived at the maximal weight, where the rigidity of the off-diagonal
sector does most of the work. We now give an estimate valid at \emph{every} weight, by an elementary
combinatorial argument that has the additional virtue of exposing the mechanism.

Call a \emph{triangle} $T$ the support --- a set of $q$ distinct modes --- of a non-zero term of $\omega$, so that
$\omega=\sum_Tc_T\,m_T$ with $c_T\ne0$. Triangles are the elementary moves available to $Q$: the operator does
nothing but add one. At $N=3$, $p=q=3$ there are $195$ of them on $27$ modes, of three kinds: $3$ with all modes
on one diagonal cell, $144$ of the form $\{(d,d),(d,e),(e,d)\}$, and $48$ with three distinct off-diagonal cells.
(Only $24$ of the $27$ flavour triples have $C_{abc}\ne0$, and cyclic rotations produce the same mode set.) The
number to keep in mind is $195$ out of $\binom{27}{3}=2925$ possible triples: the interaction hypergraph is
\textbf{sparse}, and that sparsity turns out to be the whole story.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.93\textwidth]{report_figs/g_critical.pdf}
\caption{The three cases. If a triangle is entirely empty, $Q$ can create it and $S$ is not closed. If it is
entirely occupied, $S$ could have been produced by $Q$ and the non-exactness argument fails. Only straddling
triangles avoid both.}
\label{fig:critical}
\end{figure}

Say that a set of modes $S$ is \emph{critical} if every triangle meets it partially, $0<|T\cap S|<3$ for all $T$.
Equivalently, neither $S$ nor its complement contains a triangle; equivalently again, colouring each mode by
whether it lies in $S$, no triangle comes out monochromatic. The two inequalities do two different jobs, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:critical} shows what each of them rules out.

The upper inequality makes $m_S$ closed. For $\omega_T\wedge m_S$ to be non-zero all $q$ modes of $T$ must be
absent from $S$, i.e.\ $T\cap S=\varnothing$, and criticality excludes exactly that; every term of $\omega$ is
Pauli-blocked, so $\omega\wedge m_S=0$. The lower inequality makes $m_S$ non-exact. Since $Q$ only ever adds a
triangle, the only states that can reach $S$ are those of the form $S\setminus T$ with $T\subseteq S$, so for any
$y=\sum_{S'}y_{S'}m_{S'}$ the coefficient of $m_S$ in $\omega\wedge y$ is $\sum_{T\subseteq S}\pm c_T\,y_{S\setminus T}$.
Criticality gives $T\not\subseteq S$ for every triangle, the sum is empty, and the coefficient vanishes for every
$y$ --- while $m_S$ of course has coefficient $1$ on itself. Each critical set therefore determines a non-zero
class in $H^{|S|}$, and distinct critical sets give linearly independent classes, since no combination of them
can be exact either.

This converts the question of the window into a purely combinatorial one: at which sizes do critical sets exist?
Let $\alpha$ be the size of the largest triangle-free set of modes and $\tau=n-\alpha$ the transversal number of
the triangle hypergraph, i.e.\ the size of the smallest set meeting every triangle. A set $S$ is critical exactly
when neither $S$ nor $S^c$ contains a triangle, which is to say that both are hitting sets, which is to say that
both have size at least $\tau$. Critical sets are thus confined to $\tau\le|S|\le n-\tau$, and whenever they exist
throughout that range the BPS cohomology is non-zero at $n-2\tau+1$ consecutive degrees:
\begin{equation}
W\ \ge\ n-2\tau+1\ =\ 2\alpha-n+1 .
\label{eq:taubound}
\end{equation}
Wide windows are therefore the signature of a hypergraph with large triangle-free sets --- a sparse one.

For $u(N)$ we can write the critical sets down. Let
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathbf s}=\{\text{all }p\text{ flavours of }E_{ij},\ i<j\}\ \cup\ \{s_i\text{ flavours of }E_{ii}\},\qquad
\max(0,p-q+1)\le s_i\le\min(p,q-1).
\label{eq:As}
\end{equation}
Each $A_{\mathbf s}$ is triangle-free: its strictly upper part admits no closed loop, by the index-loop argument
of \S\ref{sec:max}, and site $i$ holds only $s_i\le q-1$ flavours, one short of the $q$ that a self-loop needs.
Its complement --- the strictly lower part, acyclic for the same reason, together with $p-s_i\le q-1$ flavours per
site --- is triangle-free by the identical argument. Every $A_{\mathbf s}$ is thus critical, and as the $s_i$ range
over their allowed values $\sum_is_i$ realises \emph{every} integer in a range of length $N(2\min(p,q-1)-p)$.
Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\ W\ \ge\ N\big(2\min(p,q-1)-p\big)+1\ }
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
which at $p=1$ reads $W\ge N+1$, i.e.\ the window $\big[\tfrac{N(N-1)}2,\tfrac{N(N+1)}2\big]$ --- \textbf{exactly
Chen's single-matrix result, recovered combinatorially with no Casimir argument and no appeal to solvability}.

\label{tab:tau}

The no-go follows immediately. For $p\le q-1$ we have $W\ge pN+1$, so concentration fails for every
$N>(q-1)/p$; at Chen's values $p=q=3$ this reads $W\ge N+1>q$ for all $N\ge3$. The failure is not marginal and it
does not improve with $N$ --- it gets worse linearly.

\section{Why gauge invariance is responsible}\label{sec:gauge}

\subsection{Positive roots carry no terms}

The bound \eqref{eq:taubound} is driven entirely by $\alpha$, the largest triangle-free set, so the question
becomes why gauge theories have such large ones. The answer is a single observation about weights. Gauge
invariance means each term of $Q$ is a singlet, so the weights of its $q$ modes sum to zero. Inside
$\n^+\otimes\mathbb C^p$ --- all flavours of all positive-root vectors --- every mode carries a positive root, and
a non-empty sum of positive roots is a positive combination of simple roots, which is never zero. \textbf{No
triangle fits inside the positive-root subspace}, for any reductive $\g$ and any invariant coupling whatsoever.

Since $|\Delta^+|=\frac12(\dim\g-r)$, this alone gives
\begin{equation}
\alpha\ \ge\ p|\Delta^+|=\tfrac12(n-pr),
\end{equation}
so half of the modes of any gauge theory are automatically triangle-free. Writing $\alpha_{\rm cart}$ for the
largest triangle-free subset of the Cartan sector and feeding this into \eqref{eq:taubound}, the $|\Delta^+|$
cancels and
\begin{equation}
W\ \ge\ 2\alpha_{\rm cart}-p\,r+1 ,
\label{eq:cartan}
\end{equation}
so the window bound depends only on the rank and on what happens in the Cartan --- the root sector contributes a
fixed half and drops out. For $u(N)$ the Cartan piece is easy: a trace of $q$ diagonal matrices localises,
$\Tr[H_1\cdots H_q]=\sum_i\prod_t(H_t)_{ii}$, so a Cartan triangle needs $q$ distinct flavours at a single site
and $\alpha_{\rm cart}=\min(p,q-1)N$, which recovers \eqref{eq:main}. We have found \eqref{eq:cartan} tight in all
six measured cases.

\subsection{SYK is not obstructed}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{report_figs/g_window.pdf}
\caption{(a) Window width against gauge rank. The grading period $q=3$ is fixed, so concentration is possible only
inside the shaded band; every gauge model leaves it by $N=3$, while SYK stays flat at $q+1$. (b) The transversal
density $\tau/n$: SYK approaches $1$ (no triangle-free set larger than $q-1$), gauge models sit near $\frac12$
(the Borel half), and $\Lambda^2$ matter is worse still.}
\label{fig:window}
\end{figure}

The contrast with SYK is stark, and it is visible in the same quantity. With no gauge symmetry and generic
$q$-local couplings, \emph{every} $q$-subset of the $n$ fermions is a triangle. A triangle-free set can then have
at most $q-1$ elements, so $\alpha_{\rm SYK}=q-1$ and the estimate \eqref{eq:taubound} reads
$W\ge2(q-1)-n+1$, which is negative as soon as $n\ge2q-1$. The bound is simply \textbf{vacuous}: the argument of
\S\ref{sec:critical} imposes no obstruction at all on SYK, which is consistent with the result of Chang, Chen, Sia
and Yang that $\mathcal N=2$ SYK does concentrate, with a window of width $O(q)$ independent of $n$.

\subsection{The pinpoint}

Everything reduces to one comparison:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\rm SYK}=q-1=O(1)\qquad\text{versus}\qquad\alpha_{\rm gauge}\ \ge\ \tfrac12(n-pr)=\Theta(n),
\end{equation}
and the entire difference is the Borel subalgebra. The measured transversal densities in
Fig.~\ref{fig:window}b make the contrast concrete: $\tau/n=0.83,0.89,0.93$ for SYK at $n=12,18,27$, against
$0.44,0.46,0.47$ for $u(3),u(4),u(5)$ at $p=3$. The fraction of triples that gauge invariance permits falls like
$6/N^3$ for adjoint matter, while for SYK it is $1$.

This excludes three candidate explanations one might otherwise reach for. It is not the flavour count, since
\eqref{eq:main} holds for every $p$ and the parity pattern in $w_s$ never brings $W$ below $N+1$. It is not
randomness of the couplings: at $p=q=3$ the coupling space is a single point, and in general generic couplings are
the \emph{best} case, because matrix ranks are lower semicontinuous in $\omega$, so specialising can only enlarge
the cohomology. And it is not the loss of Casimir solvability at $p=3$, which is a symptom rather than a cause ---
indeed \eqref{eq:main} reproduces the $p=1$ solvable answer without ever using solvability. What remains is gauge
invariance itself: it forces weight-zero terms, and half of a reductive Lie algebra admits none.

There is a dichotomy lurking here that is worth stating plainly, because it explains why no amount of tuning
inside this class will help. Since $w_s\ge2$ always, a window that stays bounded as $r\to\infty$ would require
$w_s=1$, which \eqref{eq:wsbound} forbids. One may fix the rank and grow $p$, in which case $W$ does stay bounded
--- $u(2)$ with $p\equiv3$ mod $4$ has $W=3$ for every $p$, with extensive BPS entropy and a $1{:}2{:}1$ profile
--- but such a family has no matrix large-$N$ limit at all. Or one may grow the rank, obtaining the matrix limit
but with $W\ge r+1\to\infty$. Both cannot hold at once. Concentration is a property of a \emph{family} rather than
of any single model, and for a matrix model the family parameter must be the rank.

\section{Fortuity}\label{sec:fortuity}

\subsection{Chen's argument, and why it does not suffice here}

For the single-matrix model the BPS states fill the representation $r_*$, whose Young diagram is the staircase
with $2N-2i$ boxes in row $i$ and exactly $N-1$ rows. Going down in rank, that diagram has too many rows to exist
as a $U(M)$ irrep; going up, it is no longer the maximal-Casimir representation. The states therefore exist at one
rank only --- they are \emph{fortuitous} rather than monotone.

As noted in \S\ref{sec:max}, the diagram of $\lambda^*$ has rows $p(2N-2i)$, i.e.\ $p$ times Chen's staircase with
the same $N-1$ rows, so adding flavours stretches the rows without producing any new ones and his argument
generalises verbatim to all $p$. The difficulty is elsewhere: unlike the single-matrix case, our BPS states are
\emph{not} confined to one irrep. At $N=3$, $p=3$ all thirteen irreps with $C_2\ge24$ carry BPS states, each with
window width $4$. A representation-theoretic argument about $\lambda^*$ alone therefore cannot settle fortuity for
this family, and we give a local argument instead --- one that works state by state and does not care which irrep
the state sits in.

\subsection{The stabilisation map vanishes}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{report_figs/g_fortuity.pdf}
\caption{Embedding $\gl(N)\hookrightarrow\gl(N+1)$ as the upper-left block leaves every mode carrying the new
index unoccupied. The self-loop at the new site is then an entirely empty triangle, so $Q_{N+1}$ creates it and
the state is lifted.}
\label{fig:fortuity}
\end{figure}

Embed $\gl(N)\hookrightarrow\gl(N+1)$ as the upper-left block. Traces of products of block matrices restrict
correctly, so this is a chain map and induces a stabilisation map $H_N\to H_{N+1}$ on cohomology. The claim is
that this map is identically zero whenever $p\ge q$, so that no BPS state is monotone.

The reason is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:fortuity}. Under the embedding, every mode carrying the index $N+1$ is
unoccupied --- there is nothing in the new row or column. Consider the self-loop at the new site,
$T=\{\Psi^{a_1}_{N+1,N+1},\dots,\Psi^{a_q}_{N+1,N+1}\}$ with $q$ distinct flavours, which exists because $p\ge q$.
It satisfies $T\cap S=\varnothing$ for any embedded state $S$, and a triangle disjoint from $S$ is precisely one
that $Q$ can create: the Pauli blocking that made critical states closed in \S\ref{sec:critical} is absent here.
So $Q_{N+1}m_S\ne0$, the state is not closed at rank $N+1$, and it is lifted. For $p<q$ the same conclusion holds
for the explicit sets \eqref{eq:As}, using the mixed loop $i\to N+1\to j\to i$ with $i<j$: the two edges through
$N+1$ are new and hence empty, and the return edge $(j,i)$ is lower-triangular and hence empty as well. We have
verified this at $p=q=3$, where $9/9$ BPS states of rank $2$ and $27/27$ of rank $3$ are lifted, with the
disjoint triangle exhibited explicitly in each case.

It is striking that this argument and the no-go of \S\ref{sec:critical} rest on the same object --- an empty
triangle --- yet pull in opposite directions. Enlarging the gauge group manufactures a fresh site whose self-loop
is necessarily empty, and that \emph{produces} fortuity; the very same enlargement leaves the Borel half of the
algebra free of triangles, and that \emph{destroys} concentration. The two properties one wants a fortuitous
matrix model to have respond to the single available structural knob with opposite signs, which may be why the
combination has proved hard to realise.

\section{Status, and what is open}

\paragraph{Established.} The reduction \eqref{eq:wedge} of the whole problem to multiplication by a form; the
index-loop restriction and the resulting factorisation \eqref{eq:kunneth} with its width formula
\eqref{eq:Wformula}; the bound $w_s\ge2$ and hence $W\ge r+1$ at the maximal weight, \eqref{eq:wsbound}; the
closed and non-exact properties of critical states and the range \eqref{eq:taubound}; the explicit family
\eqref{eq:As} and the resulting \eqref{eq:main} for $u(N)$, together with the no-go for $N>(q-1)/p$; the
positive-root observation and \eqref{eq:cartan} for any reductive $\g$; and the vanishing of the stabilisation map.

\paragraph{Verified numerically.} All cohomology is computed by exact integer linear algebra --- Gaussian
elimination over $\mathbb F_P$, with every rank confirmed against a second prime --- using the $U(N)$ weight
decomposition to replace $2^{pN^2}$ by weight spaces of dimension at most $3\times10^4$, and Kostant inversion to
obtain per-irrep counts. Every number is cross-checked against the refined Witten index, against exact
diagonalisation at $N=2$, and against Chen's closed form at $p=1$.

\paragraph{Open.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\su(N)$.} The set \eqref{eq:As} is \emph{not} triangle-free there: the Cartan is
$(N-1)$-dimensional and spread across sites, so Cartan triangles need not localise and the counting that gave
$\alpha_{\rm cart}$ for $u(N)$ fails. The true transversal numbers are $\tau=12,21,36$ for $N=3,4,5$ at $p=3$,
giving bounds $1,4,1$ against measured widths $3,4,5$ --- valid, tight only at $N=4$, and vacuous at odd $N$,
where $\alpha=n/2$ exactly. The factorisation \eqref{eq:kunneth} also fails, the measured profiles
$13,81,81,13$ and $15,258,486,258,15$ being manifestly non-binomial. A sharp $\su(N)$ statement needs
$\alpha_{\rm cart}$ in closed form, or classes that are not single monomials.
\item \textbf{An upper bound on $W$.} Everything above bounds the window from below. The complementary statement
--- that the cohomology \emph{vanishes} outside a band, equivalently that $\omega\wedge$ is injective in low
degrees --- is verified in six models, three of them over complete spectra, but has not been established. It is
not implied by the critical-set argument, which constructs classes rather than excluding them. It is the more
valuable of the two open items: with it, the closed-form $W$ at the maximal weight would fix the window for every
irrep and the no-go would become spectrum-wide.
\item \textbf{Non-adjoint matter.} Fundamental matter forces $q=N$ (the baryon) but has $\tau=p$ independent of
$N$, hence $W\ge p(N-2)+1$: worse, not better. Two-index antisymmetric matter at $SU(6)$ gives $\tau/n=0.33$, also
worse. A general statement across representations is open.
\item \textbf{Chaos.} Nothing here bears on whether these models are chaotic. The three-flavour $H$ is not a
Casimir function, so a super-Schwarzian near-BPS spectrum remains possible without concentration.
\end{itemize}
